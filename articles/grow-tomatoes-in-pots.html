    How to Grow Tomatoes in Pots: Container Sizes, Varieties, and Watering Schedules

    By The Balcony Harvest Team Published September 24, 2026 12 min read

    Heads up: this article contains affiliate links. If you buy through them, we may earn a small commission at no extra cost to you. Read our full disclosure.

    Ninety percent of potted tomato failures come down to two things: a pot that's too small and a variety never meant for a container. Fix those two decisions and tomatoes are one of the easiest, highest-payoff crops on a balcony.

    Why Most Potted Tomatoes Fail (It's the Pot Size)

    A tomato plant is a big, thirsty machine. In the ground it spreads roots several feet wide to hunt for water and nutrients; in a pot, the root zone is whatever you give it — and most beginners give it far too little. A tomato crammed into a 1- or 2-gallon pot doesn't just grow smaller; it becomes a full-time maintenance problem. The soil dries out between breakfast and lunch, the plant stalls every hot afternoon, and fruit develops blossom end rot because the roots can't keep up.

    The other half of the failure is variety choice. Standard indeterminate tomatoes grow 6 to 8 feet tall and keep growing all season — bred for field rows, not railings. On a balcony, variety matters more than any fertilizer. Dwarf and determinate types stay compact and fruit on a schedule a container can support.

    The 7 Best Tomato Varieties for Containers (Dwarf and Determinate Only)

    Buy a transplant rather than starting seeds your first year — you'll skip 6–8 weeks of indoor babysitting. Avoid anything labeled only "indeterminate" with no size guidance: Beefsteak, Brandywine, and most heirlooms outgrow a balcony pot by July.

    1. Tiny Tim — the classic balcony tomato. 12–18 inches tall, no staking needed, sweet ¾-inch cherries in ~60 days from transplant. Fits a 2–3 gallon pot. The best first tomato for a true beginner.
    2. Patio Princess — compact determinate bred for containers. ~2 feet tall, full-size 3–4 oz fruits on a small cage. Needs 5 gallons. Excellent flavor for a container variety.
    3. Tumbling Tom (red or yellow) — cascading variety for hanging baskets. One per 10–12 inch basket, let it spill over the edge.
    4. Balcony — German-bred dwarf, 18–24 inches, made for pots and window boxes. Reliable even in cooler summers.
    5. Bush Early Girl — if you want a real slicer, this is it. Determinate, ~3 feet, fruit 54 days from transplant — one of the earliest full-size tomatoes anywhere. Needs 7+ gallons and a sturdy cage.
    6. Rosy Finch — newer dwarf from the Dwarf Tomato Project, bred for containers. Compact, 2–3 oz pink fruits, very good flavor.
    7. Husky Cherry Red — dwarf indeterminate staying 3–4 feet but producing until frost. Bigger cherries on a manageable plant. 5 gallons minimum.

    Depth matters as much as volume: aim for at least 12 inches deep. Fabric grow bags are the best balcony choice — light to carry upstairs, they air-prune roots, and excess water drains freely.

    Soil Mix and Fertilizer Schedule for Potted Tomatoes

    Fill the pot with quality bagged potting mix — never garden soil. Mix 2–3 tablespoons of slow-release organic granular fertilizer (balanced 4-4-4 or tomato-specific 3-4-6) per 5 gallons of soil into the top few inches at planting time.

    Tomatoes are heavy feeders, and a container has a finite buffet. Once the first flowers open, feed with liquid tomato fertilizer every 10–14 days at half the label rate, applied to moist soil — never feed a dry plant, it burns roots. Stop fertilizing 2–3 weeks before your expected last harvest.

    Plant the transplant deep: bury the stem up to the first set of true leaves. Tomatoes root along buried stems, so deep planting doubles the root system for free. Pinch off any leaves that would sit below the soil line.

    Watering Potted Tomatoes: A Schedule That Prevents Blossom End Rot

    Blossom end rot — the black, sunken spot on the bottom of otherwise fine fruit — is almost never a calcium deficiency in the soil. It's a watering problem: inconsistent moisture means the plant can't move the calcium it has to the fruit tips. The fix is consistency, not supplements.

    Use this as your starting schedule, then adjust with the finger test (dry 1 inch down = water):

    • Mild weather (60–70°F): 5-gallon pot — every 2–3 days. 2–3 gallon pots — every 1–2 days.
    • Summer heat (80°F+): 5-gallon pot — daily, sometimes twice on 95°F+ days. Smaller pots — twice daily in heat waves. Mulch the surface with 2 inches of straw to buy margin.
    • Always: water until it drains from the bottom, in the morning, at the base — not over the leaves. Wet foliage invites early blight, the brown-spotted disease that defoliates lower leaves by August.

    A self-watering planter with a reservoir, or a drip line on a battery timer, turns this from a daily chore into a weekly check. If you travel or work long shifts, it's the single best investment for potted tomatoes.

    Staking and Caging on a Balcony

    Even compact varieties need support once fruit loads up — a Patio Princess with 15 tomatoes on it will flop in a gust. Options that work in small spaces:

    • Compact tomato cages (28–33 in): drop one over the plant at transplant time. Caging a full-grown plant is a wrestling match you will lose.
    • Single stake + soft ties: a 4-foot bamboo stake and strips of old t-shirt. Tie loosely in a figure-eight so the stem can thicken. Best for dwarf indeterminates like Husky Cherry Red.
    • String trellis from the railing: tie garden twine to the balcony rail and spiral the main stem around it as it grows. Zero floor footprint.

    Prune suckers (shoots in leaf axils) on determinates only lightly — they set a fixed amount of fruit, and over-pruning reduces your harvest.

    FAQ

    Why are my tomato leaves turning yellow?
    Lower leaves yellowing first usually means hunger (nitrogen) or overwatering. Feed on schedule and check drainage. Yellowing at the top with green veins suggests nutrient lockout — rare in fresh bagged mix.
    Why is my tomato fruit cracking?
    Cracking follows a boom-bust watering cycle: dry spell, then heavy water, and fruit swells faster than the skin stretches. Consistent daily watering prevents it. Cracked tomatoes are still edible — eat them first.
    When should I harvest tomatoes?
    Pick at "breaker" stage — first blush of color — and ripen on the counter out of direct sun. Flavor matches vine-ripened, and you beat the birds and storms.
    Can I grow tomatoes from grocery-store seeds?
    You can, but don't your first year. Grocery tomatoes are hybrids that won't grow true, bred for shipping, not pots. A $4 transplant of a named dwarf variety outperforms a free mystery seedling every time.
