10 Easiest Herbs for Beginners (and 3 to Avoid Until Year Two)

By The Balcony Harvest Team Published September 27, 2026 8 min read

Herbs are the highest-return crop a balcony gardener can grow: one packet of basil seeds out-produces a summer of $3 store bunches. But not every herb deserves your first season. Some germinate in five days and forgive every mistake; others bolt, sulk, or never sprout — and it's not your fault. Here's the honest split: the 10 herbs that reward beginners, with exact pot sizes and watering rules, and the 3 to skip until year two.

The Ground Rules for Container Herbs

Five rules that make every herb on this list easier:

  • Minimum 6-inch pot; bigger is almost always better. Small pots dry out in a single hot afternoon. A 10–12 inch pot forgives a missed watering; a 4-inch grocery-store pot does not.
  • Potting mix, not garden soil. Garden soil compacts into a brick in containers and drowns roots. Our potting mix vs. garden soil guide explains why — and the one DIY recipe you need.
  • Drainage holes are non-negotiable. Herbs hate wet feet more than drought. No holes? Drill them, or use the pot as a cachepot with a nursery pot inside.
  • Six hours of sun for most; four for the shade-tolerant few. Mint, parsley, and chives produce with four hours of direct sun. Basil, rosemary, and thyme want six or more.
  • Buy plants for the slow ones, sow seeds for the fast ones. Perennials like thyme, oregano, and sage are cheap as nursery starts and slow from seed. Annuals like basil and dill are easy from seed — no reason to buy a plant.

The 10 Easiest Herbs, in Order of Forgiveness

1. Mint

Pot: 8-inch or larger, alone. Sun: 4+ hours; tolerates shade. Water: keep soil consistently moist.

Mint is nearly indestructible — it survives neglect, shade, and overwatering that would kill anything else. The danger is letting it take over: it spreads by underground runners and crowds out pot-mates within a season, so it always gets its own container. Snip sprigs often to keep it bushy instead of floppy. Perennial in most climates — dies back in winter, returns in spring.

2. Chives

Pot: 8-inch or larger. Sun: 4–6 hours. Water: water when the top inch is dry.

A cold-hardy perennial that asks for almost nothing and returns stronger every year. Snip the hollow leaves from the outside of the clump, leaving 2 inches of stubble — they regrow in a week or two. The purple pom-pom flowers are edible, but snipping buds early keeps leaf production going. Divide the clump every couple of years when crowded.

3. Basil (Genovese)

Pot: 10–12 inch for one plant; it gets bigger than the garden center suggests. Sun: 6–8 hours, warm weather. Water: consistently moist — never soggy, never bone-dry.

The classic beginner herb. Sow ¼ inch deep in soil above 70°F; sprouts in 5–10 days. Two rules separate thriving basil from sad basil: pinch flower buds the moment they appear (flowering turns leaves bitter), and harvest from the top down — pinching the main stem above a leaf pair grows two new stems. Afternoon wilt usually reverses within an hour of watering; a deep tray under the pot buys a buffer.

4. Parsley (flat-leaf)

Pot: 10-inch or deeper — it grows a taproot. Sun: 4–6 hours. Water: evenly moist.

Parsley is the patience test: seeds take 2–3 weeks to germinate (an overnight soak before sowing speeds things up). Then it becomes one of the toughest, most productive herbs you own — a biennial that produces for a year or more and shrugs off light frosts. Flat-leaf (Italian) parsley out-flavors curly; grow the flat kind if you cook with it. Direct-sow or buy a plant — the taproot resents transplanting.

5. Thyme

Pot: 8-inch with sharp drainage. Sun: 6+ hours. Water: let the top inch dry out; it prefers lean, dry conditions.

A Mediterranean perennial that thrives on neglect. Thyme's only real enemy is wet soil, so use a fast-draining mix and generous drainage holes. Buy a nursery plant — thyme seed is slow (often 3–4 weeks to sprout) and fiddly, and a small plant costs less than the frustration. Snip sprigs as needed; established plants handle hard cutting back in spring.

6. Oregano (Greek)

Pot: 8–10 inch. Sun: 6+ hours. Water: water when dry; drought-tolerant once established.

Another buy-the-plant perennial that's harder to kill than to grow. Greek oregano (Origanum vulgare subsp. hirtum) is the culinary workhorse — ornamental varieties look pretty but taste like dust, so check the label. It spreads, so give it its own pot. Cut it back by a third in midsummer for a flush of fresh growth.

7. Sage

Pot: 10–12 inch with good drainage. Sun: 6+ hours. Water: let dry between waterings.

A woody perennial that produces for years with minimal input. Like thyme, it wants drainage over richness — sage in soggy soil rots, in dry soil thrives. Buy a plant; seed-grown sage is slow to become useful. Harvest outer leaves as needed and prune out the woodiest stems each spring. One plant is plenty for most cooks.

8. Rosemary (buy a plant, not seeds)

Pot: 12-inch or larger, terracotta if you have it. Sun: 6–8 hours. Water: deep but infrequent — let the soil dry between waterings.

Rosemary is easy as a plant and miserable from seed — germination takes 18–21 days or more, needs soil at 75–85°F, and still comes up patchy. A nursery plant skips all of that. Once established, it wants drainage over moisture; in Zone 7 and below, bring it indoors for winter or treat it as an annual. Snip sprigs year-round.

9. Dill

Pot: 10–12 inch, deep — it grows a taproot. Sun: 6+ hours. Water: evenly moist.

Fast, fragrant, and generous — seed to harvest in about 8 weeks. The one rule: sow directly where it'll grow. Dill's taproot hates transplanting, and moved seedlings sulk for weeks. Succession-sow every 2–3 weeks, and let one plant flower at season's end to self-sow for next year.

10. Lemon balm

Pot: 8-inch or larger, alone. Sun: 4+ hours; tolerates shade. Water: keep moist.

A mint relative with bright lemon scent, and nearly as forgiving — but like mint, it spreads aggressively, so it gets its own pot. Excellent for tea, fresh or dried. Cut it back hard mid-season when leggy; it rebounds fast. Perennial in most zones.

The 3 Herbs to Avoid Until Year Two

These aren't bad herbs — their failure modes just hit beginners hardest. Skip them for one season and come back with better odds.

1. Cilantro (in warm weather)

Cilantro is a cool-season herb that bolts — flowers and turns bitter — at the first stretch of heat and long days. Plant it in May, and just when you want it for summer salsa, it's flowering. This is the most common beginner herb disappointment, and it feels like failure when it's really just timing. The fix is seasonal: sow in early spring or late summer, when cool weather keeps it leafy for weeks. Succession-sow every 2–3 weeks and choose slow-bolt varieties. Until then, buy the bunch at the store guilt-free.

2. Rosemary from seed (see #8 above)

Seed packets make it look easy, but rosemary seed is one of the slowest, patchiest germinators in the herb world. Beginners watch an empty pot for a month and conclude they can't garden. Buy the plant, enjoy the herb, sow the seeds in year two.

3. French tarragon

True French tarragon — the anise-flavored herb of béarnaise sauce — doesn't grow true from seed; it must come from cuttings or divisions. The "tarragon" seeds in most packets grow into Russian tarragon, a weedy relative that looks similar and tastes like nothing. In year two, buy a labeled French tarragon plant.

The honest math: a nursery herb plant costs about the same as two bunches of the fresh herb at the store, and one plant out-produces dozens of store bunches over a season. Buy plants for the slow perennials (thyme, oregano, sage, rosemary); sow cheap seeds for the fast annuals (basil, dill).

What to Plant Right Now (Late September)

Fall is an underrated herb season. Parsley, chives, and mint establish well in cool weather, and a September cilantro sowing gives weeks of harvest before hard frost. Perennials (thyme, oregano, sage) planted now root in before winter and return stronger in spring. Hold the basil until spring — it sulks once nights turn chilly. Feeding and Upkeep (the Short Version)

Herbs in containers need more feeding than herbs in the ground, because every watering washes nutrients out of the pot. A half-strength liquid fertilizer once a month during the growing season is plenty — overfed herbs grow lush but bland. Harvest regularly; most herbs respond to cutting by branching and producing more. When a pot stops thriving despite good care, it's often root-bound — moving up one pot size with fresh mix fixes it overnight. For the full first-season setup, see our beginner's balcony guide.

FAQ

How many herbs can I put in one pot?
Group by water needs: basil, parsley, and chives share a 12-inch pot; thyme, oregano, and sage share a drier one. Keep mint and lemon balm alone — they crowd out everything else. Never mix a thirsty herb (basil) with a drought-lover (rosemary).
Why does my grocery-store basil always die?
Those pots hold dozens of seedlings crammed together, grown fast for harvest, not for life. Separate the clump into 3–4 smaller bunches, repot each in fresh potting mix, and pinch the tops to encourage branching.
Do herbs need fertilizer?
Light feeding only. In containers, a half-strength balanced liquid fertilizer once a month during the growing season replaces what watering washes away. Too much nitrogen makes herbs grow fast and taste like nothing.
Can I grow herbs indoors year-round?
Yes, with enough light — which a windowsill alone rarely provides in winter. Mint, chives, parsley, and thyme adapt best indoors; basil and rosemary need supplemental light. Our indoor winter herb guide walks through the exact setup.
