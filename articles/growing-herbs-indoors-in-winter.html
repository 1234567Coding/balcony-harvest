    Growing Herbs Indoors in Winter: Light, Water, and Variety Guide

    By The Balcony Harvest Team Published September 14, 2026 10 min read

    Heads up: this article contains affiliate links. If you buy through them, we may earn a small commission at no extra cost to you. Read our full disclosure.

    Every October, hopeful gardeners line their brightest windowsill with pots of basil and declare victory over winter. By January, most of those plants are pale, leggy ghosts. This isn't your fault — it's physics. Here's the honest version of what it takes to keep herbs alive indoors through winter.

    The Light Problem: Why Windowsill Herbs Die in Winter

    Outdoors in summer, a sunny spot delivers 10,000+ foot-candles for 14 hours. A south-facing windowsill in December delivers maybe 500–1,000 foot-candles for 8–9 hours — a 90%+ reduction. Most herbs need 4–6 hours of strong light to grow; a winter windowsill offers survival rations at best.

    The result is predictable: plants stretch toward the window, grow pale thin leaves, and slowly decline. They don't die dramatically — they fade, which tricks growers into thinking "it's almost working" for months.

    The fix is a grow light. This is the one purchase that separates successful indoor herb growers from the windowsill-ghost crowd, and it doesn't have to be expensive — a basic full-spectrum LED setup costs less than two months of grocery-store herb clamshells.

    The 5 Herbs That Actually Survive Indoors (and 3 That Won't)

    Grow these:

    • Chives — the undisputed indoor champion. Tolerates lower light than almost anything, regrows aggressively after cutting, and goes semi-dormant rather than dying if conditions dip. Snip 2 inches above the base; they'll be back in two weeks.
    • Mint — nearly unkillable indoors. It wants consistent moisture and tolerates the lower light levels that kill fussier herbs. Keep it in its own pot (always) and harvest freely — cutting stimulates bushier growth.
    • Parsley (flat-leaf) — a biennial that happily grows through winter as a first-year plant. Slower than outdoors but steady under lights. One plant in a 1-gallon pot supplies a household.
    • Thyme — a Mediterranean native that prefers the dry indoor air most herbs hate. Needs good light and very well-draining soil (extra perlite). Once established, the lowest-maintenance indoor herb.
    • Green onions (regrown) — stick grocery-store stumps in a glass of water on the counter, harvest the tops in 10 days, repeat indefinitely. Not technically potted herbs, but the highest ROI greenery in any kitchen.

    Skip until you have serious lights:

    • Basil — the #1 indoor heartbreak. It wants heat (70°F+), intense light, and humidity. Under a windowsill it goes leggy and flavorless by February. Growable under a strong LED, but it's a project, not a windowsill plant.
    • Cilantro — bolts at the slightest warmth or light change, and indoor conditions trigger it constantly. Grow it outdoors in spring/fall instead.
    • Dill — a taprooted annual that hates transplanting and wants full sun. It sulks indoors and never really recovers. Direct-sow outdoors in spring.
    Honest take: if a recipe needs basil in January, buy the $3 grocery bunch and put your indoor effort into chives, parsley, and mint. Knowing when not to grow something is a gardening skill too.

    Grow Light Setup: Specs, Distance, and Daily Schedule

    You don't need a cannabis-grade rig. Here's the minimum effective setup:

    • Light type: full-spectrum LED (look for "full spectrum" or 4000–6500K color temperature). A 24-watt LED bar or panel covers a 2×1 ft shelf of herbs. Avoid the cheap purple "blurple" lights — they work, but they make your kitchen look like a nightclub and make it impossible to spot pests or nutrient issues.
    • Distance: 6–12 inches above the plant tops. This is the setting most people get wrong — a light 3 feet above the plants is decoration, not gardening. Hang it on adjustable chains or a simple shelf bracket so you can raise it as plants grow.
    • Duration: 14–16 hours per day on a timer. Plants need a dark period too — don't run lights 24/7.
    • Coverage: one 2-foot LED bar per shelf, or a clip-on gooseneck light per 1–2 pots. Light falls off fast at the edges, so keep the planting area matched to the light footprint.

    Electricity cost is trivial — a 24W LED running 16 hours costs roughly $1–2 per month. The timer matters more than the bulb brand: consistency beats intensity.

    Watering Indoor Herbs (Less Than You Think)

    Indoor winter air plus weaker light means plants drink far less than they did on the balcony. The #1 indoor killer isn't darkness — it's soggy soil from summer watering habits. Rules:

    • Water only when the top inch of soil is dry. In winter this might mean every 7–10 days, not every 2–3.
    • Always empty saucers 30 minutes after watering. Roots sitting in water in a cool house rot fast.
    • Use room-temperature water — cold water straight from the tap shocks roots in an already cool pot.
    • Cut fertilizer to quarter-strength monthly, or skip it entirely December–February. Plants barely growing don't need feeding, and excess salts build up in indoor pots.

    Dealing With Fungus Gnats and Low Humidity

    Fungus gnats — tiny flies hovering around indoor pots — breed in the top layer of moist soil. The fix: (1) let the top inch dry out between waterings, which breaks the breeding cycle; (2) stick yellow sticky traps in each pot to catch adults; (3) for stubborn infestations, water once with Bti (mosquito bits soaked in water), harmless to plants, pets, and people.

    Low humidity from winter heating bothers herbs less than tropical houseplants, but brown leaf tips mean the air is brutally dry. Group pots together on a pebble tray with water (the evaporating water raises local humidity), and keep plants away from heating vents. Don't mist — it does almost nothing for humidity and wets foliage, inviting disease.

    Moving Herbs Back Outside in Spring

    Don't carry winter-softened herbs straight onto a sunny balcony in April — the sun and wind will scorch them. Harden them off over 7–10 days: start with 1–2 hours of morning sun in a sheltered spot, adding an hour or two daily. Bring them in if nights drop below 45°F.

    FAQ

    Can I grow herbs indoors with no grow light at all?
    Chives and mint can survive on a bright south windowsill, growing slowly. Everything else declines over winter without supplemental light. A $25 LED bar is the difference between surviving and thriving.
    How close should the grow light be?
    6–12 inches above the plant tops for standard LED bars. Leaves bleaching or curling? Raise it. Plants still stretching? Lower it. Check monthly as plants grow.
    Why are there tiny flies in my indoor pots?
    Fungus gnats, breeding in moist topsoil. Let the top inch dry between waterings, add yellow sticky traps, and treat soil with Bti if they persist. Annoying, not dangerous.
    Should I bring my outdoor potted herbs inside for winter?
    Perennials like thyme, chives, and mint are hardy and can stay outside in most zones (mulch the pots). Tender herbs like basil and rosemary must come in before first frost — inspect for pests first, and expect a 2-week sulk while they adjust.
